# The Cost of Private Traction

*Why the infrastructure company that ships the most may still be the hardest to sell*

Between April and July 2024, Prembly — the Lagos-based identity verification company formerly known as Identitypass — shipped verification endpoints for Côte d'Ivoire, Zambia, Kenya, and a cluster of Asian markets including Singapore, Taiwan, and South Korea. In four months, the company extended its coverage from a primarily Nigerian service into a genuinely multi-continental verification layer. The engineering was formidable. The geographic ambition was clear. But a compliance officer at a European fintech evaluating African KYC providers in September 2024 would have had to dig through Prembly's own blog, past headings decorated with rocket-ship emojis, to discover that the infrastructure she needed had already been built. The traction was real. The market legibility was close to zero.

This is the tax that nobody budgets for.

A company can ship relentlessly and still be invisible to the buyers who would benefit most from what it built. The cost is not operational. It is interpretive. The work happened, but the meaning of the work never reached the people whose trust depends on understanding it. And in B2B infrastructure, where the buyer's decision is shaped by confidence in the provider's trajectory, not just its current feature set, that gap between output and meaning is not a minor inconvenience. It is a structural drag on every commercial conversation the company enters.

The instinct, especially among technical founders, is to treat the product roadmap as its own proof. The logic runs: we shipped coverage for twelve new markets; the documentation is updated; the endpoints are live. What more is there to say? The answer is: everything the prospect needs to hear before they are willing to read the documentation at all. An API changelog proves that work was done. It does not prove that the company behind it is institutionally serious, strategically coherent, or worth building a compliance dependency around. Those conclusions require a different kind of artifact — one that translates technical increment into business meaning.

The distinction matters because trust in B2B infrastructure is not verified once and stored. It is continuously re-read. Every quarter, a procurement team revisits whether this vendor is still the right bet. Every renewal cycle, a compliance officer asks whether the provider's trajectory still matches their risk appetite. The company that communicates only through documentation gives these decision-makers nothing to work with except raw technical evidence. The one that translates its progress into legible signals — structured blog posts, dated engineering retrospectives, named reliability improvements, public commitments to uptime and coverage — gives them ammunition to defend the relationship internally.

This is where Prembly's situation becomes instructive beyond its own story. The company's monthly product updates during 2024 were genuinely substantive. New endpoints for Zambian national ID verification. Facial matching for Kenyan documents. Expansion into resident card verification for Côte d'Ivoire. But the communication format — cheerful, internally-oriented, addressed to existing users rather than to prospective enterprise buyers or the broader compliance ecosystem — meant the most strategically significant quarter in the company's recent history produced almost no ambient trust outside its existing customer base.

Compare this to how Stripe treats a changelog entry. When Stripe improves API latency or adds support for a new payment method, the technical record is precise: a versioned entry with a date, a description, and a migration note. But the engineering blog post that often accompanies it does different work entirely. It explains why the improvement matters to the business layer — framing a latency reduction not as a performance metric but as a signal of infrastructure maturity. A Stripe status page update functions as ambient proof that the company's systems are actively maintained and professionally supervised. This is not a communications strategy in the traditional sense. It is an operational rhythm that compounds, over months and years, into a trust surface so thick that enterprise buyers arrive at the sales conversation already persuaded of the company's seriousness. The sales team does not have to prove that Stripe is real. The public record has already done that work.

The difference between these two approaches is not about resources or headcount. It is about translation. Prembly's engineers built something that deserved to be understood. But building and being understood are different operations, and the second one does not happen automatically because the first one was done well.

For B2B infrastructure companies — and particularly for those operating from African markets, where the trust threshold imposed by global buyers is structurally higher — this translation deficit is not a branding problem. It is a sales-cycle problem, a partnership-velocity problem, and eventually a valuation problem. The enterprise buyer who cannot find third-party evidence that a provider is growing, is serious, and is here to stay will either choose a competitor with a more legible public record or will demand more proof during the sales process, lengthening the cycle and increasing the cost of every closed deal.

The objection here is reasonable, and it deserves honest treatment. Some companies stay quiet because the work is genuinely not ready to be public. Premature narration can create expectations the product cannot yet meet, and there are seasons where heads-down execution is the correct strategic posture. The error is not in being quiet. The error is in confusing disciplined restraint with permanent absence, in treating the lack of a public signal as a neutral condition rather than an active cost that accumulates with every sales call, every partnership negotiation, and every quarterly review where the buyer's internal champion has to explain, again, why this vendor is worth the dependency.

Consider Prembly's position again, now that the argument has weight. In that four-month window in 2024, the company did not merely ship new endpoints. It completed a transformation from a Nigerian identity verification tool into a multi-continental infrastructure layer — the kind of shift that changes the category a buyer files you under. But because the translation never happened, because the public record remained a collection of product update posts aimed at existing developers, the category shift remained private. The company moved. The market's understanding of the company did not.

Traction that never becomes public meaning rarely compounds into trust. And trust, in B2B infrastructure, is not a feeling. It is a commercial asset that lowers the cost of every transaction it touches — every sales call shortened, every due diligence cycle compressed, every renewal conversation that starts from confidence rather than from scratch. The company that builds in silence pays for that silence in every room where its name comes up and nobody can point to evidence of what has changed. The tax is invisible on the income statement. But the sales team feels it in every first meeting that begins with proving the company exists, and the founder feels it in every partnership conversation that stalls because the other side cannot find a public reason to take the bet.

The hardest version of this is not that the company failed to communicate. It is that by the time the cost of silence becomes visible — in a lost deal, a declined partnership, a hire who chose the competitor with the louder public presence — the window for the communication that would have prevented it has already closed. The explanation that would have taken three sentences in a well-timed blog post now takes thirty minutes in a sales call. And thirty more in the next one.
